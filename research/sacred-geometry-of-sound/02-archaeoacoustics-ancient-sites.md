# 02 · Archaeoacoustics: The Measured Sound of Ancient Sites

This file catalogs sites where acoustic behavior has actually been **measured or formally studied**, ordered roughly by age. Each entry notes what is established, what is speculative, and where fringe interpretation diverges from the data.

---

## 2.1 Paleolithic painted caves (France/Spain, ~40,000–12,000 BP)

**Finding:** Iégor Reznikoff and Michel Dauvois (1988, caves of the Ariège) reported a statistical association between the locations of cave paintings and the most resonant points of the caves — places where a sustained voice produces strong reinforcement. Later work by Reznikoff extended this to other caves; a 2017 study by Fazenda et al. tested the hypothesis quantitatively in Spanish caves and found a *weak but present* association between motif locations and low-frequency acoustic response. `[MAINSTREAM]`

**Interpretation:** Suggestive that sound mattered in the choice of image locations; not proof of "ritual acoustics." Echoing chambers may simply have been the most impressive, habitable, or navigable spaces.

**Divergence note:** Fringe literature states flatly that "cave paintings mark resonance nodes" as settled fact. The peer-reviewed result is a modest statistical tendency with methodological caveats (selection effects, changed cave morphology over millennia).

## 2.2 Ħal Saflieni Hypogeum, Malta (~4000–2500 BCE)

The underground temple complex's "Oracle Room" is the most famous case in the field.

- **Measured:** Strong low-frequency resonant behavior; investigators associated with the 2014 Malta archaeoacoustics conference (OTS Foundation, ed. Linda Eneix) reported resonances in the region of **~110 Hz** (with a second region near 70 Hz), inside the adult male vocal range. A male voice intoning at the resonant frequency produces a dramatic building-filling effect. `[MAINSTREAM/PRIMARY]` (conference proceedings; limited formal peer review)
- **Not established:** That the chamber was *tuned* to 110 Hz deliberately, or that 110 Hz is a special "sacred frequency" (see 06 §3 for the 110 Hz claim audit).

## 2.3 Neolithic chambered cairns and passage tombs, Britain & Ireland (~3500–3000 BCE)

- **Jahn, Devereux & Ibison (1996, *JASA*):** measured standing-wave resonances in six ancient chambers (including Newgrange, Wayland's Smithy, Chun Quoit, Camster Round), finding fundamental resonances clustered at roughly **95–120 Hz** — male vocal range. `[MAINSTREAM]` — this is the anchor paper of the field, published in the flagship acoustics journal.
- **Watson & Keating (1999, *Antiquity*):** at Camster Round and the Easter Aquhorthies recumbent stone circle, showed that drumming inside chambers produces infrasound-rich standing waves and strange spatial effects for listeners, and that the stone circle geometry concentrates sound within the ring. `[MAINSTREAM]`
- **Newgrange (Ireland):** the passage-and-chamber form behaves like a resonant cavity; the winter-solstice light alignment is proven `[MAINSTREAM]`, the acoustic intentionality is plausible but unproven.

**Divergence note:** The cluster near ~110 Hz across sites is real in the measured sample. The fringe leap — "a common sacred frequency encoded worldwide" — ignores the mundane explanation: **chambers of similar size made of stone resonate at similar frequencies.** Resonant frequency is set by geometry (roughly, a few meters of chamber dimension → ~100 Hz region). Similar rooms sound similar; no shared esoteric knowledge is required. The genuinely open question is whether builders *noticed and exploited* the effect (H1).

## 2.4 Stonehenge, England (~3000–2000 BCE)

- **Trevor Cox et al. (2020, *Journal of Archaeological Science*):** built a 1:12 acoustic scale model of the complete monument ("Stonehenge Lego"). Result: the completed stone circle had a reverberation time of ~0.6–0.8 s (like a modern lecture hall — surprisingly reverberant for an open-air space), amplified speech and music *inside* the circle, and **contained** sound — poor projection outward. Suggests rituals were acoustically intimate, for those inside. `[MAINSTREAM]`
- **Rupert Till** (University of Huddersfield) produced auralizations and argued the space supports rhythmic/musical performance. `[MAINSTREAM]`

## 2.5 Greek and Roman theaters — Epidaurus (~4th c. BCE)

- **Declercq & Dekeyser (2007, *JASA*):** the famous intelligibility of Epidaurus is partly explained by the corrugated limestone seating rows acting as an acoustic filter that suppresses low-frequency background noise (wind, crowd murmur) relative to the speech band. `[MAINSTREAM]`
- Not a "sacred" mystery — but the best-documented case of ancient *empirical* acoustic engineering, and the sanctuary of Asklepios at Epidaurus was a healing sanctuary: architecture, performance, and therapeutic intent in one complex. `[PRIMARY]` (site inscriptions; Pausanias' description)

## 2.6 Chavín de Huántar, Peru (~1200–500 BCE)

The strongest case in the Americas for **deliberate** ritual acoustics.

- The temple's interior galleries are a maze of stone ducts. **Miriam Kolar and the Stanford CCRMA team** (2008–ongoing) measured the galleries and the site's famous **pututus** (Strombus conch-shell trumpets, 20 of which were excavated on site — `[PRIMARY]` artifacts). Findings: gallery geometry filters and redirects sound so its source is unlocatable; the Lanzón gallery couples with pututu frequencies; ventilation shafts function as acoustic channels. `[MAINSTREAM]`
- Combined with archaeological evidence of processional ritual and psychoactive plant use (San Pedro cactus iconography), Chavín is widely interpreted as a **purpose-built sensory-manipulation environment** for cult initiation. This interpretation is published in the mainstream literature, not fringe.

## 2.7 Chichén Itzá, Mexico — El Castillo pyramid (~9th–12th c. CE)

- **David Lubman (1998, presented to the Acoustical Society of America):** a handclap at the base of the staircase returns a **chirped echo** whose falling frequency sweep resembles the call of the resplendent quetzal — the sacred bird of Mesoamerica. The mechanism is periodic reflection from the staircase treads (a diffraction-grating-like effect, Bragg scattering). `[MAINSTREAM]` (conference paper + subsequent literature)
- Intentionality is debated: the echo is a physical consequence of any long stone staircase with regular treads; whether Maya builders tuned tread dimensions for it is plausible speculation, not demonstrated fact. Also reported: the "raindrop" sounds in the Kukulkán cenote-facing structures and speech focusing in the Great Ballcourt (measured, but intent unproven).

## 2.8 Chaco Canyon and Anasazi great kivas, US Southwest (~850–1250 CE)

Circular, semi-subterranean ceremonial rooms with measured strong axial resonances and (reconstructed) foot-drum features. Acoustic studies (e.g., by Richard Loose at Chaco) are fewer and less formal than the cases above — treat quantitative claims cautiously. `[MAINSTREAM, thin literature]`

## 2.9 Byzantine and medieval sacred architecture (532 CE onward)

- **Hagia Sophia, Istanbul:** Bissera Pentcheva's *Icons of Sound* project (Stanford, with CCRMA) measured/modeled reverberation on the order of **10+ seconds**. Byzantine chant performed in that acoustic smears into a continuous shimmering texture — the building itself becomes the instrument, which matches Byzantine theological writing about the church as heaven-on-earth. `[MAINSTREAM]` + `[PRIMARY]` (Byzantine liturgical texts)
- **Gothic cathedrals:** reverberation times of 4–10 s favor slow, melismatic, non-percussive music — a plausible co-evolution: **the architecture selected for the chant style, and the chant style was theologized as sacred.** Notre-Dame's acoustics were formally measured before and after the 2019 fire (Brian Katz's team, CNRS/Sorbonne), giving the best-documented cathedral acoustic dataset. `[MAINSTREAM]`

## 2.10 Synthesis for RQ1

| Claim | Verdict |
|---|---|
| Some ancient sacred sites have striking measured acoustics | **Established** |
| Resonances at many Neolithic chambers cluster near ~95–120 Hz | **Established (measured sample)** |
| That clustering implies a shared sacred-frequency doctrine | **Rejected** — geometry/size explains it |
| Builders sometimes deliberately engineered acoustics | **Demonstrated at Chavín (strong case); plausible at Stonehenge, Epidaurus, Hypogeum; unproven for most megaliths** |
| Acoustic effects influenced ritual experience | **Very likely** — this is H2's territory, see 04 and 07 |

The honest pattern: **intent is provable only where architecture, instruments (pututus), iconography, and acoustics all point the same way.** Chavín has all four. Most sites have only the acoustics.
